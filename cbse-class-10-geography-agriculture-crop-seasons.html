🌾 Agriculture in India: Cropping Patterns, Food Crops & Reforms

Detailed climatic requirements, harvesting calendars, and agricultural modernization initiatives.

1. The Three Cropping Seasons of India

India possesses three distinct agricultural cropping seasons based on monsoon cycles and winter cooling:

  • Kharif Season: Sown with the onset of the southwest monsoon (June–July) and harvested in autumn (September–October).
    Crops: Paddy (Rice), Maize, Jowar, Bajra, Tur (Arhar), Moong, Urad, Cotton, Jute, Groundnut, and Soyabean.
  • Rabi Season: Sown in winter (October–December) and harvested in spring/summer (April–June).
    Crops: Wheat, Barley, Peas, Gram, and Mustard. (Winter temperate cyclones from Mediterranean bring precipitation vital for wheat).
  • Zaid Season: Short season during summer months (March–June) between Rabi harvest and Kharif sowing.
    Crops: Watermelon, Muskmelon, Cucumber, Vegetables, and Fodder crops.

2. Major Food & Commercial Crops: Required Conditions

A. Rice (Principal Kharif Staple):

  • Temperature: High temperature (above 25°C) throughout growing period.
  • Rainfall: High humidity with annual precipitation above 100 cm. (Grown in low rainfall areas like Punjab/Haryana with canal irrigation).
  • Top States: West Bengal, Uttar Pradesh, Punjab, Andhra Pradesh, Odisha.

B. Wheat (Principal Rabi Staple):

  • Temperature: Cool growing season and bright sunshine at the time of ripening.
  • Rainfall: 50 to 75 cm of annual rainfall evenly distributed.
  • Top States: Uttar Pradesh, Punjab, Haryana, Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan.

C. Cotton (Fibre Crop):

  • Requires well-drained Black soil of Deccan plateau, high temperature, light rainfall, 210 frost-free days, and bright sunshine. Kharif crop taking 6 to 8 months to mature.

D. Jute (The Golden Fibre):

  • Grows well on well-drained fertile floodplains renewed annually. Requires high temperature and heavy humidity. Top producer: West Bengal. Used for gunny bags, ropes, carpets. Facing competition from synthetic nylon fibers.

3. Institutional & Technological Agricultural Reforms

Agriculture sustains over 60% of India's workforce. Crucial post-independence reforms include:

  • First Phase Reforms: Collectivization, consolidation of land holdings (Chakbandi), and abolition of the exploitative Zamindari system.
  • Green Revolution & White Revolution (Operation Flood): High Yielding Variety (HYV) seeds, chemical fertilizers, tubewell irrigation, and cooperative milk marketing.
  • Comprehensive Land Development Programme (1980s–1990s): Crop insurance against drought, flood, fire, and cyclone; establishment of Grameen Banks and Cooperative Societies for low-interest farmer loans.
  • Farmer Welfare Schemes: Kisan Credit Card (KCC), Personal Accident Insurance Scheme (PAIS), Minimum Support Price (MSP) announcements to eliminate middleman exploitation, and special weather bulletins on radio/television.
  • Bhoodan-Gramdan Movement (Bloodless Revolution): Initiated by Vinoba Bhave to persuade wealthy landlords to voluntarily donate land parcels to landless Harijan laborers.

💡 Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

❓ Differentiate between Primitive Subsistence Farming and Intensive Subsistence Farming.

Primitive Subsistence Farming is practiced on small land patches with primitive tools (hoe, dao, digging sticks) relying on family labor, monsoon rainfall, and slash-and-burn (Jhum) techniques without chemical fertilizers. Intensive Subsistence Farming is practiced in densely populated regions using high biochemical inputs and intensive tubewell irrigation to maximize per-hectare crop yields.

❓ Name two beverage crops grown in India and their optimal geographic zones.

Tea requires warm, moist, frost-free climate with frequent showers on deep, well-drained fertile loamy soils rich in humus (Assam hills, Darjeeling, Nilgiris). Coffee (specifically Arabica variety brought from Yemen) is cultivated on the Baba Budan Hills of Karnataka, Kerala, and Tamil Nadu.
